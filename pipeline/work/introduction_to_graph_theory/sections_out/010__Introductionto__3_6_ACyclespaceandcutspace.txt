Theorem 3.20(Veblen). The edge set of a multigraph G can be partitioned into the edge sets of
cycles if and only if G is even. Here loops and pairs of parallel edges count as cycles of lengths 1
and 2.

Proof. Each cycle contributes 0 or 2 to the degree of every vertex, so a multigraph partitioned
into cycles is even.

Conversely, we use induction on e(G); there is nothing to prove if e(G) ⇤0. Let G be even
with e(G) > 0, and let G0 be obtained by deleting the isolated vertices. Every vertex of G0 has
positive even degree, hence degree at least 2. If G0 has a loop or a pair of parallel edges, these
form a cycle. Otherwise G0 is a simple graph with δ(G0) ≥2, and Proposition 3.6 gives a cycle.
In either case G contains a cycle C. The multigraph G −E(C) is even and has fewer edges, so
by induction its edge set is partitioned into cycles. Adding C gives a partition for G.
⇤

Theorem 3.21(Euler). A connected multigraph with at least one edge has an Eulerian circuit if
and only if it is even.

Proof. Let W be a closed trail. Each time W passes through a vertex v, it enters along one
edge-end at v and leaves along another; the start and the ﬁnish at v0 ⇤v` likewise pair the
ends of e` and e1. Since the edges of W are distinct, W uses an even number of edge-ends at
every vertex. If W is Eulerian, it uses all edge-ends, so G is even.

Conversely, let G be connected and even with at least one edge. By Theorem 3.20, G contains
a cycle, so it has closed trails of positive length. Let W be a longest one, and suppose that W
misses an edge. Some edge f < E(W) has an endpoint v on W: otherwise no edge would join
V(W) to its complement, so V(W) ⇤V(G) by connectivity, and then every edge would belong
to W. By the ﬁrst paragraph, G −E(W) is even, so by Theorem 3.20 the edge f lies in a cycle C
of G −E(W), and C passes through v. Read W cyclically from v back to v, and then traverse C
from v back to v. This is a closed trail longer than W, a contradiction.
⇤

Corollary 3.22. A connected multigraph has an Eulerian trail that is not closed if and only if it has
exactly two vertices of odd degree. Such a trail starts and ends at these two vertices.

Proof. If x and y are the two odd vertices, add a new edge f ⇤xy. The result is connected and
even, so it has an Eulerian circuit; starting it just after f and deleting f leaves an Eulerian trail
of G joining x and y. Conversely, an Eulerian x, y-trail with x , y, followed by a new edge

f ⇤yx, is an Eulerian circuit of G + f . By Theorem 3.21, G + f is even, so x and y are exactly
the odd vertices of G.
⇤

diﬀerence: an edge appearing twice cancels.

with e is the only cycle in T + e. It is the fundamental cycle Ce.
