Proposition 4.5(DFS forest invariant). In a graph, the endpoints of every edge outside the DFS
forest are comparable in the ancestor order. A complete DFS forest is found in O(n + m) time.

Proof. Let uv be an edge outside the DFS forest, and assume that u is discovered before v. The
vertex u cannot already be ﬁnished when v is discovered. Indeed, before ﬁnishing, u would
have inspected uv while v was still undiscovered; that edge would then have discovered v and
would belong to the DFS forest. Hence u is active when v is discovered. The active vertices
form the current root-to-vertex path of recursive calls, so v is a descendant of u. Thus the
endpoints are comparable.

As in BFS, each vertex is processed once and each adjacency-list entry is inspected once.
All other work takes constant time per entry, giving total running time O(n + m).
⇤

An edge outside the DFS tree can join a vertex of a subtree to an ancestor of its root. We
next use these connections to ﬁnd bridges and cut-vertices.

Theorem 4.7(Low-link certiﬁcate; Tarjan). Let vw be an edge of the DFS tree that ﬁrst discovered

w while v was being explored.

1. The edge vw is a bridge if and only if low(w) > disc(v).

2. A non-root vertex v is a cut-vertex if and only if some child w in the DFS tree satisﬁes
low(w) ≥disc(v).

3. A root is a cut-vertex if and only if it has at least two children in the DFS tree.

All bridges and cut-vertices can therefore be found in O(n + m) time.

Proof. First consider the edge vw. By the DFS ancestor property, every edge other than vw
that joins a vertex of Tw to a vertex outside Tw must join it to v or to a strict ancestor of v. Such
an edge exists exactly when low(w) disc(v). In that case the subtree can reach the rest of the
graph without using vw, so vw is not a bridge. If no such edge exists, deleting vw separates
Tw from the rest of its component. This proves the ﬁrst assertion.

Now let v be a non-root vertex. Deleting v separates the subtree Tw of a child w from the
component of G −v containing the vertex from which v was ﬁrst discovered exactly when no
vertex of Tw has an edge outside the DFS tree to a strict ancestor of v. By the deﬁnition of the
low-link value, this is exactly the inequality low(w) ≥disc(v).

Conversely, suppose that low(w) < disc(v) for every child w of v. For each such w, some
vertex in Tw—possibly w itself—is joined by an edge outside the DFS tree to a strict ancestor
of v. After v is deleted, this edge and the remaining edges of the DFS tree connect the whole
subtree Tw to the component of G −v containing the vertex from which v was ﬁrst discovered.
Hence all child subtrees remain connected to that component, and v is not a cut-vertex. This
proves the second assertion.

Finally, distinct DFS subtrees rooted at children of a root have no edge between them.
Otherwise the later child would have been discovered while the earlier subtree was being
explored and would belong to that subtree. Deleting the root therefore separates its component
exactly when it has at least two children.

The strict and non-strict inequalities have a useful interpretation. An edge outside the DFS
tree joining a vertex of Tw to v is enough to prevent vw from being a bridge, but it disappears
when v itself is deleted. To prevent v from being a cut-vertex, the subtree must instead reach a
strict ancestor of v. The discovery and low-link values are computed during one DFS, so the
running time remains O(n + m).

When does an X, Y-bigraph have a matching covering X? Necessarily every S ✓X has at
least |S| available neighbors. Hall’s theorem says that these are the only obstructions.

Theorem 5.2(Hall’s theorem, 1935). In an X, Y-bigraph, there exists a matching covering X if
and only if |N(S)| ≥|S| for all S ✓X.

Proof. If a matching covers X, its edges inject every S ✓X into N(S), so |N(S)| ≥|S|.

We use induction to prove suﬃciency. When |X| 1, the claim is immediate. Assume
|X| > 1 and consider two cases.
