6.22-connected graphs

We ﬁrst focus on the simpler cases of 2-connected and 2-edge-connected graphs. Edge

subdivision is an important operation in both settings.

Deﬁnition 6.7. In a graph G, a subdivision of an edge uv is the operation of replacing uv
with a path u, w, v through a new vertex w.

In fact, if d(v) ≥2 or d(u) ≥2, a subdivision is a special type of vertex 2-split. Hence, edge
subdivisions preserve 2-connectedness. Moreover, the following lemma shows that it even
preserves 2-edge-connectedness.

Lemma 6.8. Edge-subdivision preserves 2-edge-connectedness.

Proof. Let G be a 2-edge-connected graph, and let H be obtained from G by adding w to
subdivide uv. Since cut-edges are precisely the edges lying on no cycle, every edge of G lies on
a cycle. The corresponding edge of H lies on the same cycle, with uv replaced by the path uwv
when necessary. Replacing uv by uwv also shows that H is connected. Thus H is connected
and has no cut-edge, so it is 2-edge-connected.
⇤

Components are the maximal connected subgraphs, and every graph is the disjoint union
of its components. We seek an analogous decomposition of a connected graph into maximal
2-connected pieces joined in a tree-like fashion. The following deﬁnition, phrased in terms of
the absence of a cut-vertex, also accommodates isolated vertices and cut-edges.

Deﬁnition 6.9. A block of a graph G is a maximal connected subgraph of G that has no
cut-vertex.

Under this deﬁnition, a block is either an isolated vertex, a cut-edge, or a maximal
2-connected subgraph. In particular, every block with more than two vertices is 2-connected.
Two blocks in a graph share at most one vertex, as otherwise their union belongs to a larger
block. Hence, the blocks of a graph partition its edge set. A vertex shared by two blocks must
be a cut-vertex of G. The block-cutpoint graph of G is the bipartite graph whose parts are the
cut-vertices and the blocks of G, with a cut-vertex adjacent to precisely the blocks containing
it. This graph is a forest. A block corresponding to a leaf is called a leaf block; it contains exactly
one cut-vertex of G.

Example: blocks and cut-vertices. Let G consist of the triangles abca and cdec, together
with the edge e f . Its blocks are the two triangles B1, B2 and the single edge B3 ⇤e f .

Deﬁnition 6.10. Let H be a graph. An ear on H is a positive-length path whose two endpoints
lie in H and whose internal vertices lie outside H.

An ear decomposition of a graph G is a sequence of edge-disjoint subgraphs Q0, . . . , Qr
whose union is G, such that Q0 is a cycle and, for each i ≥1, Qi is an ear on Q0 [ · · · [ Qi−1.

Weak ear decompositions are useful for understanding 2-edge-connected graphs. As an
application, imagine turning every street of a city into a one-way street while preserving the
ability to travel from every location to every other. This motivates the following deﬁnition.

Deﬁnition 6.13. A digraph D is strongly connected, or strong, if for every ordered pair
u, v 2 V(D) there is a directed path from u to v.

The question is which graphs admit a strong orientation. Robbins’ theorem gives a complete
characterization.

Corollary 6.14(Robbins, 1939). A multigraph with at least two vertices has a strong orientation if
and only if it is 2-edge-connected.

Proof. ()) A strong orientation has a connected underlying multigraph. If there were a
cut-edge, one could not travel both ways across its cut.

(() Use a weak ear decomposition of G. Orient the initial cycle consistently. Orient every
new open ear from one endpoint to the other, and orient every new closed ear consistently
around its cycle. In either case, each new vertex can be reached from the old strong digraph
and can reach it, so the enlarged digraph remains strong. After all ears have been added, the
resulting orientation of G is strong.

⇤

6.33-connected graphs by contraction

A vertex k-split replaces one vertex by two adjacent vertices. We now consider the inverse
operation, which contracts two adjacent vertices into one.

Deﬁnition 6.15. Given an edge e in a graph G, the contraction G/e is the graph obtained
from G by replacing the endpoints of e with a single vertex whose neighbors are their
neighbors in G outside e. We say that G/e is obtained from G by contracting e. A
k-contractible edge is an edge whose contraction leaves a k-connected graph.

Depending on the application, a diﬀerent convention for contraction may be preferable. In
a multigraph, one may retain parallel edges by making the new vertex incident with every
edge other than e that was incident with an endpoint of e. Here we work only with simple
graphs and use the preceding deﬁnition.
