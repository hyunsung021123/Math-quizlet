Proposition 3.15. Any two vertices of a tree T are joined by a unique path. Consequently, if
x, y 2 V(T) are distinct and nonadjacent, then T + xy contains exactly one cycle, formed by the
x, y-path of T and the edge xy.

Proof. Suppose that P , Q are x, y-paths in T. Some edge e ⇤ab of P is not on Q; otherwise
P would be an x, y-path inside the path Q, so P ⇤Q. Orient P so that a precedes b. The
a, x-portion of P, followed by Q and then the y, b-portion of P, is an a, b-walk in T −e.
By Proposition 3.5 it contains an a, b-path, which together with e forms a cycle in T, a
contradiction.

The x, y-path of T has length at least two, since x and y are nonadjacent, so together
with xy it forms a cycle of T + xy. Conversely, every cycle of T + xy contains xy, since T is
acyclic, and deleting xy from it leaves an x, y-path of T. By uniqueness, there is only one such
cycle.
⇤

Proposition 3.16. If G is an n-vertex graph with n −1 edges, then G is connected if and only if G
is acyclic.

Proof. If G is acyclic, every component is a tree. If its k components have orders n1, . . . , nk,
then n −1 ⇤e(G) ⇤Õk

i⇤1(ni −1) ⇤n −k, so k ⇤1.
Conversely, assume G is connected. As every n-vertex graph with at most n −2 edges is
disconnected, every edge of G is a cut-edge, so no edge lies in a cycle. Therefore G is acyclic.
⇤

We have proved that any two of “connected,” “acyclic,” and “having n −1 edges” imply
the third. Trees are therefore the minimal connected graphs on a ﬁxed vertex set.

Theorem 3.17. Every connected graph contains a spanning tree.

Proof. By Proposition 3.10, deleting an edge from a cycle does not disconnect a graph.
Repeatedly deleting cycle edges therefore leaves a connected, acyclic, spanning subgraph.
⇤

3.5Eulerian circuits

We can now answer the question about the seven bridges in Figure 1.1. Multigraphs are

allowed throughout this section, and a loop contributes two to the degree of its vertex.

Deﬁnition 3.18. An Eulerian trail in a multigraph G is a trail that uses every edge of G. A
closed Eulerian trail is an Eulerian circuit, and G is Eulerian if it has an Eulerian circuit.

Deﬁnition 3.19. A multigraph is even if every vertex has even degree.

Here “even” refers to degrees; it is unrelated to the parity of the length of a walk or a cycle.

Deleting the edges of a cycle from an even multigraph leaves an even multigraph, since a
cycle contributes exactly two to the degree of each of its vertices.

Theorem 3.20(Veblen). The edge set of a multigraph G can be partitioned into the edge sets of
cycles if and only if G is even. Here loops and pairs of parallel edges count as cycles of lengths 1
and 2.

Proof. Each cycle contributes 0 or 2 to the degree of every vertex, so a multigraph partitioned
into cycles is even.

Conversely, we use induction on e(G); there is nothing to prove if e(G) ⇤0. Let G be even
with e(G) > 0, and let G0 be obtained by deleting the isolated vertices. Every vertex of G0 has
positive even degree, hence degree at least 2. If G0 has a loop or a pair of parallel edges, these
form a cycle. Otherwise G0 is a simple graph with δ(G0) ≥2, and Proposition 3.6 gives a cycle.
In either case G contains a cycle C. The multigraph G −E(C) is even and has fewer edges, so
by induction its edge set is partitioned into cycles. Adding C gives a partition for G.
⇤

Theorem 3.21(Euler). A connected multigraph with at least one edge has an Eulerian circuit if
and only if it is even.

Proof. Let W be a closed trail. Each time W passes through a vertex v, it enters along one
edge-end at v and leaves along another; the start and the ﬁnish at v0 ⇤v` likewise pair the
ends of e` and e1. Since the edges of W are distinct, W uses an even number of edge-ends at
every vertex. If W is Eulerian, it uses all edge-ends, so G is even.

Conversely, let G be connected and even with at least one edge. By Theorem 3.20, G contains
a cycle, so it has closed trails of positive length. Let W be a longest one, and suppose that W
misses an edge. Some edge f < E(W) has an endpoint v on W: otherwise no edge would join
V(W) to its complement, so V(W) ⇤V(G) by connectivity, and then every edge would belong
to W. By the ﬁrst paragraph, G −E(W) is even, so by Theorem 3.20 the edge f lies in a cycle C
of G −E(W), and C passes through v. Read W cyclically from v back to v, and then traverse C
from v back to v. This is a closed trail longer than W, a contradiction.
⇤

Corollary 3.22. A connected multigraph has an Eulerian trail that is not closed if and only if it has
exactly two vertices of odd degree. Such a trail starts and ends at these two vertices.

Proof. If x and y are the two odd vertices, add a new edge f ⇤xy. The result is connected and
even, so it has an Eulerian circuit; starting it just after f and deleting f leaves an Eulerian trail
of G joining x and y. Conversely, an Eulerian x, y-trail with x , y, followed by a new edge

f ⇤yx, is an Eulerian circuit of G + f . By Theorem 3.21, G + f is even, so x and y are exactly
the odd vertices of G.
⇤

In the multigraph of Figure 1.1, the land masses A, B, C, D have degrees 5, 3, 3, 3. All four
are odd, so by Theorem 3.21 and Corollary 3.22 there is no walk that crosses each bridge
exactly once.

3.6[A] Cycle space and cut space

Adding an edge to a spanning tree creates one cycle. This simple observation also gives a way
to describe all even-degree subgraphs at once. The useful addition operation is symmetric
diﬀerence: an edge appearing twice cancels.
