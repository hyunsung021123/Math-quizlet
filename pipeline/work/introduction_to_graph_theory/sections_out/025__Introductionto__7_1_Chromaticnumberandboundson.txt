7.1Chromatic number and bounds on it

Recall the frequency assignment problem. In this problem, we used graphs so that the edges
represent ‘conﬂicts’. To use as few frequency channels as possible, we seek the minimum
number of conﬂict-free classes. This can be formalized using the following concepts.

Deﬁnition 7.1. A k-coloring of G is a vertex labeling f : V(G) ! S where |S| ⇤k. A
k-coloring f is proper if f (x) , f (y) when xy 2 E(G). A graph G is k-colorable if it
has a proper k-coloring. The chromatic number χ(G) is the minimum k such that G is
k-colorable. If χ(G) ⇤k, then G is a k-chromatic graph.

In order to obtain an upper bound on the chromatic number, we need to come up with a
way to color the vertices to ﬁnd a proper coloring. One obvious way is the greedy choice of
colors.

Algorithm 7.2. The greedy coloring with respect to a vertex ordering v1, . . . , vn of V(G)
colors vertices in the order v1, . . . , vn, giving vi the least indexed color not already used
on its earlier neighbors.

In fact, using this greedy coloring algorithm, we obtain the following upper bound on χ(G).

Proposition 7.3. χ(G) ∆(G) + 1.

Proof. In any vertex ordering, each vertex has at most ∆(G) earlier neighbors, so the greedy
coloring will not use more than ∆(G) + 1 colors.
⇤

This bound is attained by complete graphs and odd cycles. However, if we explore other
graphs, we often see that the inequality is not tight for them. Indeed, we will later characterize
the connected graphs satisfying equality (Theorem 7.28).

In this greedy algorithm, we consider an arbitrary vertex ordering. In particular, in the
proof of Proposition 7.3, we used no special property of the vertex ordering. What if we take
a ‘good’ vertex ordering? Can we obtain a better upper bound? What vertex orderings are
good? In order to obtain a better bound using the same proof as Proposition 7.3, the important
thing is to minimize the number of ‘earlier neighbors’ in the ordering. For this purpose, the
following concept naturally arises.

Deﬁnition 7.4. A graph G is k-degenerate if every nonempty subgraph has a vertex of
degree at most k. The degeneracy of G is maxú,H✓G δ(H); that is, the minimum k such that
G is k-degenerate. A smallest-last ordering of an n-vertex graph G is constructed iteratively
from index n to 1 by letting vi be a vertex of minimum degree in G −{vi+1, . . . , vn}.

For example, a k-regular graph has degeneracy k, and any tree with at least one edge is
1-degenerate. Using this concept, the proof of Proposition 7.3 yields the following better
bound.

Proposition 7.5. If G is k-degenerate, then G is (k + 1)-colorable.

Proof. In a smallest-last ordering, vi has at most k neighbors among v1, . . . , vi−1. Greedy
coloring in this order therefore uses at most k + 1 colors.
⇤

The degeneracy plus one is called the coloring number. It need not equal the chromatic
number. For example, a complete bipartite graph Kn,n has degeneracy n, but it is 2-colorable:
color all vertices in one part with one color and all vertices in the other part with another color.

7.2Chromatic number and orientations

What other concepts are relevant to the chromatic number? If a graph G is colored with a

coloring f using colors 1, . . . , k, then we can orient each edge uv with f (u) < f (v) from u to
v. In this orientation, every directed path has length at most k −1. This shows that the lengths
of directed paths in an orientation of the graph are relevant to the chromatic number. In fact,
the following theorem provides an upper bound on χ(G) in terms of the orientations of G.

Theorem 7.6(Gallai–Hasse–Roy–Vitaver theorem). For every orientation D of G,

χ(G) 1 + `(D),

G.
