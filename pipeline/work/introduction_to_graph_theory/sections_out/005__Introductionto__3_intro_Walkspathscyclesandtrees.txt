Connections in graphs

In a network, a sequence of incident edges records a possible route. Several closely related
notions distinguish whether vertices or edges may be repeated.

Deﬁnition 3.1. A walk in a multigraph G is an alternating sequence

v0, e1, v1, e2, . . . , e`, v`,

where ei has endpoints vi−1 and vi. Its length is `, the number of edge occurrences in the
sequence. The same convention deﬁnes the length of a trail, path, circuit, or cycle. A walk,
and likewise each of these more restricted objects, is even or odd according as its length is
even or odd. The walk is closed if v0 ⇤v`, a trail if its edges are distinct, and a path if its
vertices are distinct. A closed trail is a circuit; a nonempty closed trail whose only repeated
vertex is v0 ⇤v` is a cycle.

The explicit edge sequence matters in a multigraph because two consecutive vertices may
be joined by parallel edges. It also allows vi−1 ⇤vi when ei is a loop. In a simple graph we
usually suppress the edge symbols and write only v0v1 . . . v`.

We also regard a path or cycle as the subgraph formed by its vertices and edges. We write
Pn for the path on n ≥1 vertices and Cn for the cycle on n ≥3 vertices. Thus Pn has length
n −1, whereas Cn has length n.

Returning to connectivity in networks, the following deﬁnition formalizes the notion of a
connection.

Deﬁnition 3.2. A graph G is connected if it is nonempty and every pair u, v 2 V(G) is joined
by a path. A component of G is a maximal connected subgraph.

For sets or subgraphs with a given property, minimal and maximal mean inclusion-minimal
and inclusion-maximal: no proper subset or subgraph, respectively no proper superset or
supergraph among those under consideration, has the property. In contrast, minimum and
maximum compare sizes, or another speciﬁed quantity such as length or weight, among all
eligible objects. Thus a component need not be a largest connected subgraph.

The single vertex u is a path of length 0 from u to itself. Thus G is connected if and only if
it has exactly one component.

Deﬁnition 3.3. For graphs G ⇤(V, E) and H ⇤(V0, E0), their disjoint union is (V tV0, EtE0),
where disjoint copies of V and V0 are understood.

Every graph is the disjoint union of its components. Consequently, many structural questions
reduce to connected graphs.

Proposition 3.5. Every walk from u to v contains a path from u to v.

Proof. Starting from a u, v-walk W, repeatedly delete the closed portion between two occur-
rences of the same vertex. The endpoints remain u and v, and the length strictly decreases at
each deletion. The process terminates with a walk having no repeated vertex, which is the
required path.
⇤

The same deletion argument works for directed walks and directed paths.

Lemma 3.6. Every odd closed walk contains an odd cycle.

Proof. If the walk is not a cycle, split it at two occurrences of a repeated vertex other than the
initial/ﬁnal pair. The portion between these occurrences and the remaining portion, read
cyclically, are shorter closed walks. Their lengths sum to an odd number, so one is odd. Repeat
with that walk. The length strictly decreases, so the process ends with an odd cycle.
⇤

This yields the fundamental characterization of bipartite graphs.

Theorem 3.7(Odd-cycle characterization). A graph is bipartite if and only if it has no odd cycles.

Proof. A walk in a bipartite graph alternates between the two parts, so every closed walk, and
in particular every cycle, has even length.

Conversely, suppose that G has no odd cycle. In each component C, choose a root u 2 V(C)
and partition the vertices according to the parity of the length of a shortest u, v-path. A vertex
cannot admit both an even and an odd u, v-walk, because following one and then the other
in reverse would give an odd closed walk and Lemma 3.6 would give an odd cycle. The
endpoints of every edge have opposite parities, so the two parity classes form a bipartition of
C. Taking their unions over all components gives a bipartition of G.
⇤

Graph parameters and structural properties often constrain one another. The next elementary
result relates minimum degree to long paths and cycles.

Proposition 3.8. Every graph G with minimum degree δ(G) ⇤δ ≥2 contains a path of length δ
and a cycle of length at least δ + 1.

Proof. Let v1 . . . vk be a longest path of G. Every neighbor of vk lies in {v1, . . . , vk−1}; otherwise
the path could be extended. Hence k −1 ≥d(vk) ≥δ, and the path has length at least δ.

Let i be the least index for which vivk 2 E(G). All neighbors of vk lie in {vi, . . . , vk−1}, so
k −i ≥δ. Therefore vivi+1 . . . vkvi is a cycle of length k −i + 1 ≥δ + 1.
⇤

Both bounds are sharp: Kδ+1 has minimum degree δ, longest path length δ, and longest
cycle length δ + 1.

These two results are elementary examples of two broad styles of graph-theoretic questions.
In structural graph theory, one seeks exact descriptions of graph classes: the odd-cycle theorem
characterizes bipartite graphs by the obstructions they exclude. In extremal graph theory,
one asks how a numerical hypothesis forces a prescribed structure: the minimum-degree
proposition gives sharp guarantees for a long path and a long cycle.

The following notions record single points of failure.

Deﬁnition 3.9. A cut-vertex or cut-edge of G is a vertex or edge whose deletion increases the
number of components. A cut-edge is also called a bridge.

The following proposition relates connectivity to cycles.

Proposition 3.10. If e is an edge of a connected graph G, then G −e is connected if and only if e
belongs to a cycle in G. Equivalently, adding an edge e to a graph H creates no cycle if and only if
the endpoints of e lie in distinct components of H.

Proof. Suppose that e ⇤xy 2 E(G). If G −e is connected, then an x, y-path in G −e, together
with e, forms a cycle.

Conversely, suppose that e belongs to a cycle C. Choose u, v 2 V(G) and a u, v-path P in
G. If P avoids e, then P ✓G −e. Otherwise, assume that u, x, y, v occur in this order on P.
Concatenating the u, x-subpath of P, the x, y-path C −e, and the y, v-subpath of P gives a
u, v-walk in G −e. Hence G −e is connected.
⇤

Proposition 3.11. A graph with n vertices and m edges has at least n −m components.

Proof. Start with the empty graph on the same vertex set and add the m edges one at a time.
Initially there are n components, and each added edge decreases the number of components
by at most one. At least n −m components remain.
⇤

Thus every connected n-vertex graph has at least n −1 edges. Equality is attained by the
path Pn and by the star K1,n−1. We now characterize all equality cases.

Deﬁnition 3.12. A forest is an acyclic graph, and a tree is a connected forest. A spanning tree
of a graph is a spanning subgraph that is a tree.

A vertex of degree 1 is a leaf; a vertex of degree 0 is isolated.

Proposition 3.13.
1. Every nontrivial tree has at least two leaves.

2. Deletion of a leaf from a tree yields a tree with one fewer vertex.

3. Every n-vertex tree has n −1 edges.

Proof. Let G be a nontrivial tree. Since G is connected, it has no isolated vertices.

Let P be a maximal path. If an endpoint u of P had degree at least two, then an edge
incident with u other than the edge of P would join u to another vertex of P; this would create
a cycle. Thus both endpoints of P are leaves.

A leaf cannot be a cut-vertex: deleting it leaves every path between the other vertices intact.
Deleting a vertex cannot create a cycle, so deleting a leaf from a tree leaves a connected acyclic
graph, hence a tree.

Build the tree from n isolated vertices by adding its edges. No added edge creates a cycle,
so each one reduces the number of components by exactly one. Reducing n components to
one takes n −1 edges.
⇤

Theorem 3.15. Every connected graph contains a spanning tree.

Proof. By the preceding proposition, deleting an edge from a cycle does not disconnect a graph.
Repeatedly deleting cycle edges therefore leaves a connected, acyclic, spanning subgraph.
⇤
