Figure 1.1. The seven bridges join four land masses A, B, C, D. Replacing each land mass by a vertex
and each numbered bridge by an edge preserves exactly the incidence information relevant to Euler’s
question. Parallel bridges become parallel edges.

In the eighteenth century, the city of Königsberg inspired a celebrated puzzle: can one take
a walk that crosses each of its seven bridges exactly once? The geometry of the islands and the
lengths of the bridges do not matter. What matters is only which land masses are joined by
which bridges. Replacing each land mass by a vertex and each bridge by an edge produces the
graph in Figure 1.1. Euler’s answer, which depends only on the degrees of this multigraph,
appears in Section 3.5.

This act of abstraction is typical. A transit map suppresses geographic detail while preserving
which stations are connected. A communication network records which devices can exchange
data. A social network records a chosen relation between people. A graph is a mathematical
model for precisely this kind of pairwise relational information.

The main themes of the course arise from the questions we ask about such models.

Connectivity.. Can information, traﬃc, or ﬂow travel between two speciﬁed vertices? How
many vertices or edges must fail before the network disconnects? Paths, cycles, cuts, and
Menger’s theorem make these questions precise.

Matching.. Can jobs be assigned to qualiﬁed applicants, students to projects, or donors to
compatible recipients without conﬂicts? A matching selects pairwise disjoint feasible pairs.
Hall’s and Tutte’s theorems reveal the obstructions to perfect assignments.

Coloring.. How can conﬂicting objects be assigned diﬀerent resources? Examples include exam
scheduling and frequency allocation. A color represents a resource, and adjacent vertices must
receive diﬀerent colors.

Planarity and topology.. The four-color problem asks for a coloring of a map in which neighbor-
ing regions receive diﬀerent colors. Translating maps into graphs leads to planar embeddings,
Euler’s formula, graph minors, and the interaction between graph theory and topology.

Probabilistic and extremal methods.. Random choices can prove that an object exists even when
no explicit construction is apparent. Extremal graph theory asks how many edges force a
prescribed subgraph. Together these viewpoints explain many striking threshold and Ramsey
phenomena.

Spectral methods and flows.. The adjacency relation can be encoded by a 0–1 matrix, bringing
linear algebra into graph theory. Orientations and nowhere-zero ﬂows provide a second
language for conservation and reveal a duality with coloring in plane graphs.
