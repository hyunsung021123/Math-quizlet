Deﬁnition 6.23. For X, Y ✓V(G), an X, Y-path meets X only at its ﬁrst vertex and Y only
at its last vertex. In a digraph it is directed from X to Y. A path of length zero at a
vertex of X \ Y is allowed. An X, Y-separator is a set Z ✓V(G) meeting every X, Y-path.
Unlike an x, y-separating set, it may contain endpoints; in particular, both X and Y are
X, Y-separators.

Theorem 6.24(Menger’s theorem, set form). In a graph or digraph G, the minimum size of an
X, Y-separator equals the maximum number of pairwise vertex-disjoint X, Y-paths.

Proof. Every separator meets the disjoint paths in distinct vertices, so only the existence of
enough paths needs proof. We use induction on |V(G)| + |E(G)|. Let k be the minimum size of
an X, Y-separator. For k ⇤0 there is no X, Y-path, and the assertion is immediate. Assume
that k ≥1.

Case 1. There is a minimum X, Y-separator Z diﬀerent from both X and Y. Let G1 be induced
by Z and all vertices on X, Z-paths, and let G2 be induced by Z and all vertices on Z, Y-paths.
These graphs meet only in Z: an intersection outside Z would give a walk from X to Y avoiding

Z, and hence an X, Y-path avoiding Z. Moreover, no vertex of Y \ Z belongs to G1, and no
vertex of X \ Z belongs to G2. Both diﬀerences are nonempty, since |Z| ⇤k min{|X|, |Y|}
and Z , X, Y. Thus both graphs have fewer vertices than G.

Every X, Y-path contains an X, Z-path before its ﬁrst vertex in Z, and a Z, Y-path after its last
vertex in Z. Consequently, any separator between X \ V(G1) and Z in G1 is an X, Y-separator
in G. Its minimum size is therefore k, since Z itself is one such separator. Induction supplies k

every vertex of Z exactly once. Join the two paths at each vertex of Z; since the graphs meet
only in Z, this gives k disjoint X, Y-paths.

Deﬁnition 6.27. For x 2 V(G) and U ✓V(G) \ {x}, an x, U-fan of size k is a family of k
paths from x to U that pairwise meet only at x and meet U only at their other endpoints.

Connectivity guarantees the existence of such fans. We ﬁrst record a simple consequence of
the deﬁnition of k-connectedness; its proof is left as an exercise.

Lemma 6.28(Expansion lemma). If G is a k-connected graph and G0 is formed from G by adding
a vertex y with at least k neighbors in G, then G0 is k-connected.

Lemma 6.29(Fan lemma; Dirac 1960). A graph with more than k vertices is k-connected if and
only if, for every x 2 V(G) and every U ✓V(G) \ {x} with |U| ≥k, it has an x, U-fan of size k.

Proof. Suppose ﬁrst that G is k-connected. Add a new vertex y adjacent to every vertex of U.
By the expansion lemma the resulting graph is k-connected, so Menger’s theorem gives k
independent x, y-paths. Truncate each path at its ﬁrst vertex in U.

Conversely, suppose that G is not k-connected. Choose a separating set S with |S| < k and
vertices x, y in distinct components of G −S. Since |V(G)| > k, we may enlarge S, without
using x or y, so that |S| ⇤k −1. Set U :⇤S [ {y}. An x, U-fan of size k would have to use
every vertex of U as an endpoint. In particular, its path ending at y would have to avoid S
internally, contradicting that every x, y-path meets S.
⇤

A cycle in an n-vertex graph has length at most n. A cycle of length n is spanning, which
motivates the following deﬁnition.

Deﬁnition 6.31. A spanning cycle is called a Hamiltonian cycle. A graph is Hamiltonian if it
contains a Hamiltonian cycle.

The earlier minimum-degree bound guarantees a cycle of length at least δ(G) + 1. It yields
a Hamiltonian cycle when δ(G) ⇤n −1, but this covers only the complete graph. Dirac’s
theorem gives the much stronger threshold δ(G) ≥n/2.

Corollary 6.32(Dirac’s theorem, 1952). Let G be an n-vertex graph. If n ≥3 and δ(G) ≥n/2,
then G is Hamiltonian.

We prove the following stronger result.

Corollary 6.33(Ore’s theorem, 1960). Let G be an n-vertex graph. If n ≥3 and d(u) + d(v) ≥n
for all distinct nonadjacent vertices u, v, then G is Hamiltonian.

Both bounds are sharp. The complete bipartite graph Kb(n−1)/2c,d(n+1)/2e shows that Dirac’s
minimum-degree threshold cannot be lowered. For Ore’s threshold, identify one vertex of
Ka+1 with one vertex of Kb+1, where a, b ≥1 and a + b ⇤n −1. Every nonadjacent pair in the
resulting graph has degree sum n −1, but the common vertex is a cut-vertex, so the graph is
not Hamiltonian.

6.6[A] Closure and Chvátal’s degree-sequence theorem

We now deﬁne the closure operation used to apply Ore’s lemma repeatedly.

Deﬁnition 6.35. A (Hamiltonian) closure of an n-vertex graph G is obtained by repeatedly
joining nonadjacent vertices whose current degree sum is at least n, until no such pair
remains. A graph is closed if no such pair exists.

Theorem 6.36(Bondy–Chvátal, 1976). The closure of a graph is unique; that is, it does not depend
on the order of edge additions. Denote it by C(G). A graph is Hamiltonian if and only if its closure
is Hamiltonian.

Proof. From an n-vertex graph G, form a closure G1 by adding e1, . . . , er in order and a closure
G2 by adding f1, . . . , fs in order. Each addition joins vertices whose degree sum is at least n
at that time. Once a pair becomes addable, it remains addable and therefore must be joined
before the process terminates.

If G2 * G1, let fi be the ﬁrst edge in the second sequence that is absent from G1. Since
f1, . . . , fi−1 2 E(G1), the edge fi is addable to G1 and therefore must also belong to G1, a
contradiction. Hence G2 ✓G1; by symmetry, G1 ✓G2.

The second assertion follows from Ore’s lemma.
⇤
