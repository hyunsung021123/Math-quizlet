7.11[A] Perfect graphs

Proper vertex coloring and edge-coloring both have the following form: partition a set S into
the fewest classes such that no class contains two elements from any of the sets Q1, . . . , Qt
contained in S. For vertex coloring, S is the vertex set and the Qi are cliques (or simply edges).
For edge-coloring, S is the edge set and the Qi are sets of edges having a common endpoint.

From this viewpoint, the coloring problem is a covering problem rather than a partition
problem. It is enough to ﬁnd suitable classes whose union covers all the elements, because we
can arbitrarily delete repeated elements from the classes to obtain a partition.

The corresponding packing problem asks for the largest set of elements no two of which
can belong to the same color class. For vertex coloring, such sets are cliques. For edge-coloring
of a graph, they are sets of pairwise incident edges, which form a star or a triangle. Each color
class covers at most one element of such a set, so the size of every packing is a lower bound
for the number of color classes.

For the paired optimization problems considered here, this comparison is called weak
duality: every feasible solution of the minimization problem has value at least that of every
feasible solution of the maximization problem.

A min-max relation is a statement that equality holds for the answers to two dual integer
optimization problems for some family of instances. For example, the equality ↵0(G) ⇤β(G) for
bipartite graphs is a min-max relation. Often, such a relation leads to eﬃcient algorithms for
the corresponding optimization problems. Min-max relations do not always hold for coloring
and edge-coloring, but they do hold for some special graph classes.

Deﬁnition 7.47. A clique covering of a graph G is a partition of V(G) into cliques; we view
a coloring of G as a partition of V(G) into independent sets. We consider four parameters,

Naturally, χ(G) ≥!(G) and ✓(G) ≥↵(G). Note that ✓(G) ⇤χ(G) and ↵(G) ⇤!(G) for the

equality for all induced subgraphs.

Deﬁnition 7.48. A graph is perfect if χ(H) ⇤!(H) for every induced subgraph H of G.

Deﬁnition 7.49. A graph class G is hereditary if every induced subgraph of a graph in G is
also in G.

Deﬁnition 7.50. A hole or chordless cycle in a graph G is a cycle of length at least four
having no chord, i.e. it is an induced subgraph which is a cycle of length at least four. A
graph is a chordal graph if it has no hole.

Deﬁnition 7.51. A vertex is simplicial if its neighborhood is a clique. A simplicial (or perfect)
elimination ordering is an ordering vn, . . . , v1 in which vertices can be deleted so that
each vertex vi is a simplicial vertex of the graph induced by {v1, . . . , vi}.

These two concepts of chordal graphs and simplicial elimination ordering are closely related
as follows.

Theorem 7.52. A graph G is chordal if and only if it has a simplicial elimination ordering.

Proof. If G has a chordless cycle C, then the ﬁrst vertex deleted from C in an elimination ordering
has nonadjacent remaining neighbors. Hence such a graph has no simplicial elimination
ordering.

Since the family of chordal graphs is hereditary, it suﬃces to prove by induction that every
chordal graph has a simplicial vertex. To facilitate the induction, we prove the following
stronger statement: if a chordal graph is not a complete graph, then it has two nonadjacent
simplicial vertices.

Let x and y be nonadjacent vertices in G. Let S be a minimal set of vertices whose deletion
separates x and y.

We ﬁrst prove that S is a clique. Let u and v be distinct vertices of S. By the minimality of S,
both u and v have neighbors in the components A and B of G −S containing x and y. The
union of shortest u, v-paths through A and B is a cycle of length at least 4. By the choice of the
paths and the absence of edges from A to B, this cycle has no chord other than uv. Since G has
no chordless cycle, uv 2 E(G). Since u, v 2 S were chosen arbitrarily, S is a clique.

Now consider the graphs G1 ⇤G[S [ A], and G2 ⇤G[S [ B]. Each is a chordal graph. If
one of them is complete, then any vertex outside of S is simplicial. Otherwise, Gi has two
nonadjacent simplicial vertices by induction hypothesis. Since S is a clique, at least one of them
is outside S. However, a vertex in V(Gi) −S has no neighbors in G outside V(Gi). Therefore,
vertices u1 and u2 outside S that are simplicial in G1, G2 are nonadjacent and simplicial in

G.
⇤

The reverse of an elimination ordering is a ‘construction’ ordering. Every chordal graph can be
obtained from K1 by iteratively adding a new vertex whose neighborhood is a clique. Using
this, we can show that all chordal graphs are perfect.

Theorem 7.53. Chordal graphs are perfect.

Proof. Since the chordal graphs form a hereditary family, we need only prove that χ(G) ⇤!(G).

Every chordal graph has a simplicial elimination ordering. We apply greedy coloring
relative to the reverse of such an ordering. If v receives color k, then the colors 1, . . . , k −1
appear on the earlier neighbors of v. Since they form a clique, together with v they form a
clique of size k. Thus we obtain a clique whose size equals the number of colors used.
⇤

Proposition 7.54. The following classes of graphs are perfect.

(a) Bipartite graphs.

(b) Line graphs of bipartite graphs.

(c) The complements of line graphs of bipartite graphs.

(d) The complements of bipartite graphs.

Proof. (a) The graph with no vertices has χ(H) ⇤!(H) ⇤0. Every nonempty induced subgraph
H of a bipartite graph satisﬁes χ(H) ⇤!(H): both values are 1 when H has no edge, and both
are 2 otherwise.

(b) Line graphs of bipartite graphs also form a hereditary class. A coloring of L(G) is an
edge-coloring of G, and when G is bipartite and has an edge, the cliques of L(G) come from
stars, so !(L(G)) ⇤∆(G). Thus König’s line-coloring theorem (Theorem 7.40), χ0(G) ⇤∆(G)
for bipartite G, implies that line graphs of bipartite graphs are perfect.

(c) An independent set in L(G) is a matching in G. Every clique of L(G) is a star or
comes from a triangle, and a bipartite graph has no triangle. Thus a clique cover of L(G)
corresponds to a vertex cover of G. The König–Egerváry theorem (Theorem 5.7) gives
↵(L(G)) ⇤↵0(G) ⇤β(G) ⇤✓(L(G)), so the complement of the line graph is perfect.

(d) In a bipartite graph G, a minimum clique partition consists of edges from a maximum
matching and singleton vertices, so ✓(G) ⇤|V(G)| −↵0(G). By the König–Egerváry theorem
this equals |V(G)| −β(G) ⇤↵(G). Hence G is perfect; the same argument applies to every
induced subgraph.
⇤

This proposition suggests that the complement of a perfect graph should also be perfect.
Berge conjectured this statement, and Lovász proved it in 1972; it is now called the Perfect
Graph Theorem.

If an n-vertex graph H is !(H)-colorable, then by the pigeonhole principle, some inde-
pendent set has at least n/!(H) vertices, and hence !(H)↵(H) ≥n. Thus perfection of a
graph G or its complement implies that !(H)↵(H) ≥|V(H)| for all induced subgraphs H of
G. Lovász proved the converse. A short linear-algebraic proof was later found by Gasparian.
The following deﬁnition and lemma are useful for presenting it.

Deﬁnition 7.55. A graph G is p-critical, or minimally imperfect, if G is not perfect but every
proper induced subgraph of G is perfect.

Lemma 7.56. If S is an independent set in a p-critical graph G, then !(G) ⇤!(G −S).

Proof. If S ⇤ú, the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise, since G is p-critical, we have
χ(G) > !(G) and χ(G −S) ⇤!(G −S). Since G can be properly colored by adding S as one
color class to an optimal coloring of G −S, we have

χ(G −S) + 1 ≥χ(G) > !(G) ≥!(G −S) ⇤χ(G −S).

Since all quantities are integers, χ(G) −1 ⇤!(G) ⇤!(G −S).
⇤

Corollary 7.58(Perfect Graph Theorem; Lovász, 21083). A graph is perfect if and only if its comple-
ment is perfect.

Proof. Induced subgraphs of G are the complements of induced subgraphs of G, and ↵(H) ⇤
!(H) and !(H) ⇤↵(H). Hence the condition in Theorem 7.57 holds for G if and only if it
holds for G.
⇤

An odd hole is an induced odd cycle of length at least 5, and an odd antihole is the complement
of an odd hole. Berge also conjectured the Strong Perfect Graph Theorem: a graph is perfect if
and only if it contains neither an odd hole nor an odd antihole. Equivalently, the minimally
imperfect graphs are precisely the odd holes and odd antiholes. The self-complementary
formulation implies the Perfect Graph Theorem. Chudnovsky, Robertson, Seymour, and
Thomas announced a proof in 2002; the full paper appeared in 2006 [CRST06].
