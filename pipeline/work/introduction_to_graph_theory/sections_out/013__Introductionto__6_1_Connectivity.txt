6.1Connectivity

We saw that trees are minimally connected graphs. We now ask how to quantify higher levels

of connectivity. The following deﬁnitions measure how resistant a graph is to the deletion of
vertices or edges.

Deﬁnition 6.1. A separating set or vertex cut of a graph G is a set S ✓V(G) such that G −S
has more than one component. A graph G is k-connected if it has more than k vertices
and every separating set has size at least k. The connectivity of G, written (G), is the
maximum k such that G is k-connected.

The convention for complete graphs deserves mention. Since Kn has no separating set,
the condition on its order is decisive: Kn is k-connected precisely when k n −1, and
hence (Kn) ⇤n −1. Although this convention may initially seem artiﬁcial, it makes general
statements about connectivity work uniformly; for example, deleting one edge reduces
vertex-connectivity by at most one.

connected if G −F is connected for every F ✓E(G) with |F| < k. Its edge-connectivity
is

Vertex-connectivity and edge-connectivity are both natural measures of robustness. For
edge-connectivity, it suﬃces to consider edge cuts, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 6.3. Every minimal set of edges whose deletion disconnects a graph is an edge cut.

Proof. Let F be minimal such that G −F is disconnected, and let S be the vertex set of a
component of G −F. Every edge between S and S belongs to F, so [S, S] ✓F. Since deleting
[S, S] already disconnects G, the minimality of F gives F ⇤[S, S].
⇤

Minimal here means that no proper subset of F disconnects the graph; F need not have the
smallest size among all disconnecting edge sets.

One way to disconnect a graph is to isolate a vertex, either by deleting its neighbors or by
deleting its incident edges. This observation relates vertex-connectivity, edge-connectivity,
and minimum degree.
