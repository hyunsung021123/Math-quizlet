Theorem 5.23(Berge, 1957). A matching M in a graph G is maximum if and only if G has no

M-augmenting path.

Proof. An augmenting path immediately produces a larger matching, as observed above.
Conversely, suppose that G has a matching M0 with |M0| > |M|, and consider the spanning
subgraph with edge set M4M0. Every component is a path, an even cycle, or an isolated
vertex, and its edges alternate between M and M0. Since M0 has more edges in total, some
path component has one more edge of M0 than of M. It begins and ends with edges of M0 and
is therefore an M-augmenting path.
⇤

The theorem leads to a concrete search algorithm in bipartite graphs.

Algorithm 5.24(Bipartite augmenting-path search). Let G have bipartition (X, Y) and current
matching M. Let U ✓X be the uncovered vertices. Initialize S :⇤U and T :⇤ú, and mark
every vertex of X as unscanned.

While some vertex x 2 S is unscanned, scan every edge xy < M with y < T. Add y to
T and record x as its predecessor. If y is uncovered, follow the predecessor pointers back
to U to recover an augmenting path, augment M, and restart the search. If y is matched
to w 2 X, add w to S and record y as its predecessor. After all such edges at x have been
considered, mark x as scanned.

If every vertex of S is scanned without reaching an uncovered vertex of Y, stop: no
augmenting path exists, and (X \ S) [ T is a minimum vertex cover.

Theorem 5.25. Repeated application of Algorithm 5.24 produces a maximum matching M and a
vertex cover of size |M|.

Proof. At an unsuccessful search, every nonmatching edge from S ends in T, and every
matching edge from S also ends in T. Thus no edge joins S to Y \ T, so (X \ S) [ T is a vertex
cover.

Every vertex of S \ U is the mate of a unique vertex of T, and every vertex of T is matched;
otherwise the search would have found an augmenting path. Hence |T| ⇤|S| −|U|. Since M
covers precisely X \ U,

|(X \ S) [ T| ⇤|X| −|S| + |T| ⇤|X| −|U| ⇤|M|.

A matching cannot be larger than a vertex cover, so both are optimal.
⇤

After isolated vertices are set aside, each search takes O(m) time, and each successful
search increases |M| by one. There are at most n/2 successful searches, giving an O(n + mn)
implementation, where n ⇤|V(G)| and m ⇤e(G). Hopcroft and Karp (1973) ﬁnd many
shortest augmenting paths simultaneously and run in O((m + n)pn) time.

Odd cycles complicate the same search in nonbipartite graphs. Edmonds’ blossom algorithm
contracts certain odd alternating cycles, called blossoms, while preserving the existence of
augmenting paths; the contracted graph need not be bipartite. Micali and Vazirani (1980) later
gave an O(mpn) algorithm for general graphs after isolated vertices are set aside.

Sometimes feasibility is not enough: preferences also matter. Consider two sets X and Y of
equal size n, with each vertex having a strict preference order over the vertices in the other set.

Deﬁnition 5.26. A perfect matching is stable if it has no blocking pair: an unmatched pair
(x, y) 2 X ⇥Y such that both x and y prefer one another to their assigned partners.

Algorithm 5.27(Gale–Shapley deferred-acceptance algorithm). Initially every vertex is free.
While some free x 2 X has not proposed to every vertex of Y, let x propose to the

highest-ranked y to whom it has not yet proposed. If y is free, y tentatively accepts x.
Otherwise, y keeps the preferred of the current partner and x, rejecting the other. Stop
when no proposer in X is free.

Theorem 5.28(Gale–Shapley, 1962). The deferred-acceptance algorithm terminates after at most

n2 proposals and produces a stable perfect matching.

Proof. No ordered pair (x, y) is considered twice, so at most n2 proposals occur. Once a vertex
of Y receives a proposal, it remains tentatively matched and can only exchange its partner
for a more preferred one. Because the two parts have equal size and every preference list is
complete, termination leaves no vertex free, so the matching is perfect.

Suppose that (x, y) blocks the ﬁnal matching. Since x prefers y to its ﬁnal partner, x
proposed to y earlier. The vertex y rejected x (at that moment or later) only in favor of someone
it preferred to x, and its tentative partner could only improve thereafter. Thus y cannot prefer
x to its ﬁnal partner, a contradiction.
⇤

The proposing side receives a stronger guarantee. Write M(x) for the partner of x in a
perfect matching M. A stable matching M is X-optimal if, for every stable matching N and
every x 2 X, either M(x) ⇤N(x) or x prefers M(x) to N(x). Thus each vertex of X gets its best
partner among all stable matchings, not necessarily among all perfect matchings.

Corollary 5.29(X-optimality). With X proposing, the deferred-acceptance algorithm produces the
unique X-optimal stable matching. In particular, its output does not depend on the order in which
free vertices of X propose.

Proof. We ﬁrst show that no pair belonging to a stable matching is ever rejected. Otherwise,
consider the ﬁrst such rejection: y rejects x in favor of x0, and let N be a stable matching
containing xy. Write y0 ⇤N(x0); then y0 , y. If x0 preferred y0 to y, it would have proposed to

y0 and been rejected before proposing to y. Since x0y0 2 N, this contradicts the choice of the
ﬁrst rejection. Hence x0 prefers y to y0. But y prefers x0 to x, so (x0, y) blocks N, a contradiction.

Now let M be the output. Every vertex that x prefers to M(x) has already rejected x, and
therefore cannot be its partner in any stable matching. Thus M is X-optimal. Strict preferences
make each vertex’s best possible stable partner unique, so the matching is unique as well.
⇤

This proposer-optimality is also part of the original Gale–Shapley theorem [GS62].

6.1Connectivity

We saw that trees are minimally connected graphs. We now ask how to quantify higher levels

of connectivity. The following deﬁnitions measure how resistant a graph is to the deletion of
vertices or edges.

Deﬁnition 6.1. A separating set or vertex cut of a graph G is a set S ✓V(G) such that G −S
has more than one component. A graph G is k-connected if it has more than k vertices
and every separating set has size at least k. The connectivity of G, written (G), is the
maximum k such that G is k-connected.

The convention for complete graphs deserves mention. Since Kn has no separating set,
the condition on its order is decisive: Kn is k-connected precisely when k n −1, and
hence (Kn) ⇤n −1. Although this convention may initially seem artiﬁcial, it makes general
statements about connectivity work uniformly; for example, deleting one edge reduces
vertex-connectivity by at most one.

Deﬁnition 6.2. Let G be a multigraph with at least two vertices. We say that G is k-edge-
connected if G −F is connected for every F ✓E(G) with |F| < k. Its edge-connectivity
is

0(G) :⇤max{k : G is k-edge-connected}.

Vertex-connectivity and edge-connectivity are both natural measures of robustness. For
edge-connectivity, it suﬃces to consider edge cuts, as the next proposition shows.

Proposition 6.3. Every minimal set of edges whose deletion disconnects a graph is an edge cut.

Proof. Let F be minimal such that G −F is disconnected, and let S be the vertex set of a
component of G −F. Every edge between S and S belongs to F, so [S, S] ✓F. Since deleting
[S, S] already disconnects G, the minimality of F gives F ⇤[S, S].
⇤

Minimal here means that no proper subset of F disconnects the graph; F need not have the
smallest size among all disconnecting edge sets.

One way to disconnect a graph is to isolate a vertex, either by deleting its neighbors or by
deleting its incident edges. This observation relates vertex-connectivity, edge-connectivity,
and minimum degree.
