5.3Augmenting paths

A matching is maximal if no edge can be added to it, and maximum if it has size ↵0(G). These
notions diﬀer: on P6, the second and fourth edges form a maximal matching of size two,
although a matching of size three exists. The following notion explains how a matching that is
not maximum can be improved.

Deﬁnition 5.19. Given a matching M, an M-alternating path alternates between edges in M
and edges outside M. An M-augmenting path is a positive-length alternating path whose
two endpoints are uncovered by M.

For two edge sets A and B, their symmetric diﬀerence is A4B :⇤(A \ B) [ (B \ A). If P is
M-augmenting, then

M0 :⇤M4E(P) ⇤(M \ E(P)) [ (E(P) \ M)

is a matching with |M0| ⇤|M| + 1. Indeed, the ends of P are uncovered by M, so the ﬁrst and
last edges of P lie outside M, and P has one more edge outside M than in M. An internal
vertex of P is covered by exactly one edge of M, and that edge lies on P; hence in M0 it is
covered only by its other edge on P. The two ends of P are covered only by their edges on P.

Theorem 5.20(Berge, 21068). A matching M in a graph G is maximum if and only if G has no

M-augmenting path.

Proof. An augmenting path immediately produces a larger matching, as observed above.
Conversely, suppose that G has a matching N with |N| > |M|, and consider the spanning
subgraph with edge set M4N. Every vertex meets at most one edge of each matching, so every
component is a path, an even cycle, or an isolated vertex, and its edges alternate between M
and N. Since N has more edges in total, some path component has one more edge of N than
of M. It begins and ends with edges of N. Its two ends are uncovered by M: an edge of M at
an end would lie in M4N, because the end’s only N-edge is on the path and is not in M, so
the end would have degree two there. Hence this component is an M-augmenting path.

5.4[A] Factors beyond perfect matchings

Using Tutte’s 1-factor theorem, we can deduce a theorem regarding more general factors. The
following deﬁnition provides a concept more general than k-factors.

Deﬁnition 5.21. Given a multigraph G and f : V(G) ! N0, an f -factor of G is a spanning
subgraph H such that dH(v) ⇤f (v) for all v 2 V(G); as usual, a loop contributes two to
dH(v).

We can reduce the f -factor problem to a perfect-matching problem. If f (v) > dG(v)
for some v, there is clearly no f -factor, so assume f (v) dG(v) for every vertex and set

Index the vertices of A(v) by the edge incidences at v. For every edge, join the two vertices
corresponding to its two incidences. (A loop has two distinct incidences at the same vertex.)
Call the resulting graph H.
