Proposition 1.10. For a nonempty topological space X, the following are equivalent:
(1) X is irreducible.
(2) Every nonempty open subset of X is dense.
(3) Any two nonempty open subsets of X meet.
Proof. If a nonempty open subset U ⊆X is not dense, then the proper closed subsets
X \U and U cover X. Thus (1) implies (2). If U and W are nonempty open subsets of X
and U is dense in X, then U meets W. Thus (2) implies (3). Finally, if X = X1 ∪X2 with
X1, X2 proper and closed, then X \ X1 and X \ X2 are disjoint nonempty open subsets.
Thus (3) implies (1).
□
Let X be an irreducible topological space, and let P be a property about a point of
X. We say that P holds for a general point of X if the locus
XP := {x ∈X | x has property P}
contains a nonempty open subset of X.
Equivalently, the locus where P fails is
contained in a proper closed subset of X. The set XP itself is not required to be open.
Every nonempty open subset occurring here is dense by Proposition 1.10. Moreover,
finitely many properties each of which holds for a general point hold simultaneously
for a general point: if U1, . . . , Us are corresponding nonempty open subsets, then
U1 ∩· · · ∩Us is nonempty and open by repeated applying Proposition 1.10. In this
course, a general object always has this open-set meaning with respect to a specified
irreducible parameter space. In other words, a general object is contained in a dense
open subset in the irreducible parameter space. A very general object is contained in a
countable intersection of dense open subsets in the irreducible parameter space.

Example 1.11. The lexicographic order with x1 ≻x2 ≻· · · ≻xn compares exponent
vectors at the first place where they differ. The degree reverse lexicographic order
first compares total degree and, among equal degrees, declares the monomial with the
smaller exponent at the last differing place to be larger. Both are monomial orders,
but they can give different leading monomials.

Theorem 1.18. Every ideal I ⊆Rn has a finite Gr¨obner basis.
Proof. By Dickson lemma, the monomial ideal in≺(I) has finite monomial generators
m1, . . . , ms. For each mi, we can choose gi ∈I with LM≺(gi) = mi. Then G := {g1, . . . , gs}
is a Gr¨obner basis of I.
□
Corollary 1.19 (Hilbert Basis Theorem). Every ideal of Rn is finitely generated.
Proof. This immediately follows from Theorem 1.18 and Corollary 1.17.
□
Hilbert basis theorem says that if X ⊆An is an affine algebraic set, then there are
finitely many polynomials f1, . . . , fs ∈Rn such that X = V( f1, . . . , fs).
Corollary 1.20. For an ideal I ⊆Rn, the residue classes of the monomials not in in≺(I) form a
C-vector space basis of Rn/I.
Proof. Choose a Gr¨obner basis G of I. By Proposition 1.14, the residue classes of the
monomials not in in≺(I) span Rn/I. If a nonzero linear combination of such monomials
belongs to I, then its leading monomial lies in in≺(I) and is one of the chosen monomials,
a contradiction. Hence the residue classes are linearly independent.
□

A Gr¨obner basis G of an ideal I ⊆Rn is reduced if every g ∈G is monic and no
monomial of g is divisible by LM≺(h) for any h ∈G \ {g}.
Proposition 1.24. Every nonzero ideal I ⊆Rn has a unique reduced Gr¨obner basis.
Proof. Start with a finite Gr¨obner basis G of I. Delete every element whose leading
monomial is divisible by another leading monomial, until the remaining leading
monomials are the minimal monomial generators of in≺(I). Make the elements monic.
For each g ∈G, replace it by LM≺(g) + RG\{g}(g −LM≺(g)). This replacement remains in
I, and does not change the generated ideal. Minimality of mi ensures that the leading
monomial survives. Thus the resulting set G is a reduced Gr¨obner basis of I. Now,
suppose that G and H are two reduced Gr¨obner bases of I. Note that the minimal
monomial generators of a monomial ideal are unique. Thus, for each g ∈G, there is
a unique h ∈H with the same leading monomial. Since both are monic, the leading
terms cancel in g −h ∈I. Every remaining monomial in g −h is outside in≺(I) by
reducedness. By Corollary 1.20, we get g −h = 0. Repeating the arguments, we finally
obtain G = H.
□

Theorem 1.26 (Elimination Theorem). Use lexicographic order x1 ≻x2 ≻· · · ≻xn on Rn.
Let I ⊆Rn be an ideal. For each 1 ≤j ≤n −1, set
Ij := I ∩C[xj+1, . . . , xn].
If G is a Gr¨obner basis of I, then
Gj := G ∩C[xj+1, . . . , xn]
is a Gr¨obner basis of Ij for every 1 ≤j ≤n −1.
Proof. For f ∈Rn, we have f ∈C[xj+1, . . . , xn] if and only if LM≺(f) ∈C[xj+1, . . . , xn].
Choose 0 , f ∈Ij. Since G is a Gr¨obner basis of I, there is g ∈G such that LM≺(g)
divides LM≺(f). The latter monomial contains none of x1, . . . , xj, so neither does the
former. Thus g ∈Gj. This shows that the leading monomials of Gj generate in≺(Ij).
□
