2.3. Rational functions. Let X be an affine variety. For nonempty open subsets U, V ⊆
X and f ∈OX(U), g ∈OX(V), we define (U, f) ∼(V, g) if and only if f = g on some
nonempty open subset W ⊆U ∩V. It is easy to check that this is an equivalence
relation. An equivalence class of such pairs is called a rational function on X. Let
C(X) be the set of rational functions. We define addition and multiplication on C(X)
in a usual way. Then they are well-defined, and make C(X) a C-algebra. We call C(X)
the function field of X.

Let X, Y, Z be affine varieties. A rational map ϕ: X d Y is dominant if the image
of one representative is dense in Y. In this case, the image of every representative
is dense in Y. If ϕ: X d Y is dominant and ψ: Y d Z is a rational map, then their
composition ψ ◦ϕ is also a rational map.
Theorem 2.16. Let X, Y, Z be affine varieties. Then we have the following:
(1) Dominant rational maps ϕ: X d Y correspond bijectively to injective C-algebra homo-
morphisms ϕ∗: C(Y) →C(X).
(2) If ϕ: X d Y and ψ: Y d Z are dominant rational maps, then (ψ ◦ϕ)∗= ϕ∗◦ψ∗.

assertion (2) is clear.

Affine varieties X and Y are birational, written X ∼bir Y, if there are dominant
rational maps ϕ: X d Y and ψ: Y d X such that ψ ◦ϕ = idX and ϕ ◦ψ = idY as
rational maps. In this case, ϕ is called a birational map.
Corollary 2.17. For affine varieties X and Y, the following are equivalent:
(1) X ∼bir Y.
(2) The function fields C(X) and C(Y) are isomorphic as C-algebras.
(3) There are nonempty open subsets U ⊆X and V ⊆Y that are isomorphic by mutually
inverse regular maps.
Proof. Theorem 2.16 shows (1) ⇐⇒(2), and (1) ⇐⇒(3) is clear.
□
