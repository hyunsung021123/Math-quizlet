4.3. Segre maps. It is not obvious how to endow the product of two quasi-projective
varieties with a quasi-projective structure. If one naively equips Pn × Pn with the box
topology, then the diagonal
∆Pn := {(x, x) | x ∈Pn} ⊆Pn × Pn
is not closed. Indeed, every open neighborhood of every point of Pn × Pn intersects
∆Pn, so ∆Pn = Pn × Pn. To give the product a quasi-projective structure, one instead
considers the Segre embedding.
A polynomial in variables x0, . . . , xm and y0, . . . , yn is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(a, b) if it is homogeneous of degree a in the x-variables and degree b in the y-variables.
Its zero locus is well defined on Pm×Pn. Give P(m+1)(n+1)−1 coordinates Zij with 0 ≤i ≤m
and 0 ≤j ≤n. The Segre map is

Proposition 4.13. Every projective algebraic set in Pm × Pn is exactly the common zero locus
of bihomogeneous polynomials in C[x1, . . . , xm, y0, . . . , yn].
Proof. Every closed subset of Σm,n is Σm,n ∩V+(I) for some homogenous ideal I ⊆Sm,n.
Then the ideal (Ψm,n(I)) ⊆C[x1, . . . , xm, y0, . . . , ym] is generated by bihomogenous poly-
nomials, and ψm,n(Σm,n ∩V+(I)) is the common zero locus of such polynomials. Con-
versely, we need to show the the common zero locus of bihomogeneous polynomials
F1, . . . , Fr ∈C[x1, . . . , xm, y0, . . . , yn] is a closed subset of Pm × Pn. It is enough to show
that the zero locus of a nonzero bihomogenous polynomial F of bidegree (a, b) is
closed. Put e := max{a, b}, and multiply F by every x-monomial of degree e −a and
every y-monomial of degree e −b. The resulting polynomials are all bihomogenous
of bidegree (e, e), and their common zero locus is the zero locus of F. If e = 0, then F
is a nonzero constant and its zero locus is empty. Assume e > 0. Every monomial of
bidegree (e, e) is a product of e terms xiyj. After substituting Zij = xiyj, each resulting
polynomials is a homogeneous polynomial of degree e in the Segre coordinates. Its
zero locus is closed in Σm,n, so the zero loci of the resulting polynomials are closed in
Pm × Pn. The zero locus of F is the intersection of the finitely many such zero loci, so it
is also closed.
□

4.4. Products of algebraic varieties. For quasi-projective varieties X ⊆Pm and Y ⊆Pn,
we define
X × Y := σm,n(X × Y) ⊆Σm,n.
By Proposition 4.13, we can naturally identify Am × An with Am+n.
Theorem 4.16. Let X ⊆Am and Y ⊆An be affine varieties. Then X × Y ⊆Am × An =
Am+n is an affine variety with I(X × Y) = I(X)Rm,n + I(Y)Rm,n ⊆Rm,n, where Rm,n :=
C[x1, . . . , xm, y1, . . . , yn].
Proof. First, we make a useful observation. For f ∈Rm,n, we can choose q1, . . . , qr ∈
C[y1, . . . , yn] whose residue classes form a basis of the span of all the nonzero mono-
mials in y occurring f in C[y1, . . . , yn]/I(Y). Then [f] = Pr
i=1 aiqi in C[y1, . . . , yn]/I(Y) for
some a1, . . . , ar ∈C[x1, . . . , xm]. For p ∈X, we have f(p, y) = 0 for every y ∈Y if and only
if ai(p) = 0 for every 1 ≤i ≤r by the strong Nullstellensatz. Next, we show that X × Y
is irreducible. Suppose that X × Y = Z1 ∪Z2 for closed subsets Z1, Z2 ⊆X × Y. By the
first observation, one can show that Si := {p ∈X | {p}×Y ⊆Zi} ⊆X is closed for i = 1, 2.
Since {p} × Y  Y is irreducible, {p} × Y is contained in Z1 or Z2. Thus X = S1 ∪S2, so
X = S1 or S2. Then X×Y = Z1 or Z2. Finally, it is clear that I(X)Rm,n+I(Y)Rm,n ⊆I(X×Y).
The reverse inclusion can be shown by the above observation and the Strong Nullstel-
lensatz.
