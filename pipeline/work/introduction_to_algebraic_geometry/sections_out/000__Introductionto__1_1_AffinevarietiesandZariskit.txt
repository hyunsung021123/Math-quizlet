The ground field is always C, and we write Rn := C[x1, . . . , xn] for simplicity.
1.1. Affine varieties and Zariski topology. We set An := Cn. For a subset F ⊆Rn, the
affine zero locus is
V(F) := {p ∈An | f(p) = 0 for every f ∈F} ⊆An.
Note that V(F) = V((F)).
Here (F) is the ideal of Rn generated by F.
We write
V(f1, . . . , fs) for V({f1, . . . , fs}). We have V(0) = An and V(1) = ∅. A subset X ⊆An is an
affine algebraic set if X = V(F) for some F ⊆Rn. For a subset Y ⊆An, the vanishing
ideal of Y is
I(Y) := {f ∈Rn | f(p) = 0 for every p ∈Y} ⊆Rn.
It is easy to see that I(Y) ⊆Rn is an ideal and Y ⊆V(I(Y)).

The Zariski topology on An is the topology whose closed subsets are the affine
algebraic sets. This is a genuine topology on An. Indeed, ∅and An are closed, finite
unions of closed subsets are closed, and arbitrary intersections of closed subsets are
closed by Proposition 1.1. An affine n-space is An equipped with the Zariski topology.
If X ⊆An is an algebraic set, then X has the induced Zariski topology: its closed
subsets are the sets X ∩V(J) with J ⊆Rn an ideal.

A nonempty topological space X is irreducible if it cannot be written as X = X1 ∪X2
with both X1 and X2 proper closed subsets of X. It is reducible otherwise. An affine
variety is an irreducible affine algebraic set equipped with the Zariski topology.
Proposition 1.5. A nonempty affine algebraic set X is irreducible if and only if I(X) is a prime
ideal.
Proof. Suppose X is irreducible and f g ∈I(X). If neither f nor g belongs to I(X), then
X ∩V(f) and X ∩V(g) are both proper closed subsets. But their union is X, so we get a
contradiction. Thus I(X) is prime. Conversely, suppose I(X) is prime and X = X1 ∪X2
with X1, X2 closed in X. If Xi is proper, then V(I(Xi)) = Xi , X = V(I(X)) so that
I(X) ⊊I(Xi). If X1, X2 are proper subsets of X, then we can choose f ∈I(X1) \ I(X) and
g ∈I(X2) \ I(X). But f g vanishes on X, so we get a contradiction to that I(X) is prime.
Therefore, one of X1, X2 equals X.
□

Example 1.9. Note that V(xy) = V(x) ∪V(y) ⊆A2 is the union of the two coordinate

Proposition 1.10. For a nonempty topological space X, the following are equivalent:
(1) X is irreducible.
(2) Every nonempty open subset of X is dense.
(3) Any two nonempty open subsets of X meet.
Proof. If a nonempty open subset U ⊆X is not dense, then the proper closed subsets
X \U and U cover X. Thus (1) implies (2). If U and W are nonempty open subsets of X
and U is dense in X, then U meets W. Thus (2) implies (3). Finally, if X = X1 ∪X2 with
X1, X2 proper and closed, then X \ X1 and X \ X2 are disjoint nonempty open subsets.
Thus (3) implies (1).
□
Let X be an irreducible topological space, and let P be a property about a point of
X. We say that P holds for a general point of X if the locus
XP := {x ∈X | x has property P}
contains a nonempty open subset of X.
Equivalently, the locus where P fails is
contained in a proper closed subset of X. The set XP itself is not required to be open.
Every nonempty open subset occurring here is dense by Proposition 1.10. Moreover,
finitely many properties each of which holds for a general point hold simultaneously
for a general point: if U1, . . . , Us are corresponding nonempty open subsets, then
U1 ∩· · · ∩Us is nonempty and open by repeated applying Proposition 1.10. In this
course, a general object always has this open-set meaning with respect to a specified
irreducible parameter space. In other words, a general object is contained in a dense
open subset in the irreducible parameter space. A very general object is contained in a
countable intersection of dense open subsets in the irreducible parameter space.
